Human-in-the-Loop Is a Feature, Not a Failure

Neolith Team · September 9, 2026 · 3 min read

The instinct to remove every human from the process is what turns a working agent into an incident. Routing the hard cases to people is how the easy ones get automated safely.

There is a moment in most automation projects when someone asks whether the system can handle a case "without involving a person". The honest answer is usually that it can, and that it should not. Escalation is not evidence that the agent failed. It is the mechanism that makes a limited agent safe to deploy.

Confidence is not correctness

Language models are fluent when they are right and equally fluent when they are wrong, which means the agent's own stated confidence is a poor signal. What works better is external: does the retrieved record actually exist, did the tool return a result in the expected shape, do two sources agree. Route to a human when those checks fail, not when the model sounds unsure.

Pick the escalation points deliberately

  • Irreversible actions. Anything that moves money, cancels an order or sends an external message should have a confirmation step — even if it is a fast one.
  • Low-confidence retrieval. No matching record, several plausible matches, or data older than it should be.
  • Frustration and repetition. A caller or user who asks the same thing twice is telling you the agent is not being understood. Stop looping and hand over.
  • Defined sensitive topics. Complaints, anything with legal or medical weight, and anything the business has decided is not an automated conversation.

Make the handoff carry its context

The most expensive handoff is the one that starts from zero. If a person takes over and the first thing they do is ask for the account number the system already looked up, you have doubled the work and halved the goodwill. Pass the gathered context — who is asking, what was attempted, what came back, what remains undecided — into the queue. This is a data problem, not an interface problem, and it is usually the difference between an escalation that feels like help and one that feels like being passed along.

Escalations are your training set

Every escalated case is a labelled example of something the system could not yet do. Review them on a schedule and sort them into three piles: cases to automate next, cases that reveal a missing tool or a data problem, and cases that should always go to a human. That third pile is a legitimate, permanent category. Not every decision should be automated, and saying so out loud early saves a great deal of rework later.

Measure the right thing

Containment rate — the share of interactions the agent completes unaided — is the metric everyone quotes, and on its own it creates the wrong incentives: it rewards an agent that refuses to escalate. Pair it with escalation quality instead. How often does a handoff actually need the human's judgement? How long does the customer wait across the whole chain? An agent that resolves eighty per cent of cases and hands the other twenty over cleanly will beat one that grinds through ninety-five per cent and loses the customer on the remainder.

Automate the routine. Escalate the rest on purpose. That is not a limitation of the design — it is the design.
