Start With a Workflow Audit, Not a Model

Neolith Team · October 7, 2026 · 3 min read

The most expensive AI mistake is automating the wrong process beautifully. A day of mapping usually beats a month of prompting.

The first question in most AI projects is "which model should we use?". It should be "which process should we touch?". Model choice is reversible in an afternoon; the choice of process sets the ceiling on the whole programme.

Why the audit comes first

Automation multiplies whatever it is applied to. Point it at a well-defined, high-volume, rules-heavy process and you get compounding savings. Point it at a process that exists only because two systems were never integrated, or that four people each perform slightly differently, and you get an expensive machine for producing inconsistency faster.

You also cannot estimate the return without the map. The value of automating a task is roughly frequency multiplied by handling time multiplied by the share of cases that are genuinely routine — and that last factor is the one nobody knows until they look.

What a useful audit collects

  • The trigger. What starts the process, and how often it actually fires. Weekly assumptions are often daily reality.
  • The steps and their owners. Including the undocumented ones — the person who re-checks a number by hand before it goes out.
  • Where the waiting is. Most process time is queue time, not work time. AI helps with work; integration helps with queues.
  • Where the judgement is. The steps that need a human decision are exactly where a human-in-the-loop design belongs, not the steps that merely look complicated.
  • Failure cost and blast radius. What happens when the step is wrong, and who finds out. This sets the guardrails before anything ships.

Scoring candidates

Once the steps are on the page, rank them on three axes: volume, how rule-based the decision is, and how cheap it is to verify the output. High volume plus rule-based plus easy verification is the starting zone — it produces a working system in weeks and generates the trust needed for harder work later. Low volume plus judgement-heavy plus hard to verify is where projects go to die, regardless of how impressive the demo looked.

The roadmap falls out of the audit

A good audit ends with a phased plan, and the phases should be honest about sequence. First, a narrow deployment that returns something measurable. Second, the integration work that lets the agent reach real systems of record, because an agent that cannot act is a chatbot. Third, expansion to adjacent workflows once the first one has been running long enough to trust. Attempting phase three first is the most common way these programmes stall.

What it changes in practice

Teams that audit first tend to discover that their automation opportunity is not where they assumed. It is rarely the task everyone complains about — the loud task is usually loud because it is genuinely hard. It is more often the quiet, high-frequency, low-glory task that one person does forty times a week and nobody has ever written down.

Find that task. Then pick the model.
